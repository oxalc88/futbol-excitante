# Visual Direction & Technical-Art Strategy for Stylized 11v11 Football

The game should adopt a bold, graphic style inspired by *Mark of the Ninja*: high-contrast silhouettes, controlled palettes, and clear shapes rather than realism.  Players and elements will use simplified geometry and deliberate exaggeration of form to ensure instant readability, especially at a distance or during fast motion.  Color and light will be used sparingly to highlight team identity and game-critical objects (ball, goals) against the mid-toned environment.  Overall, the tone is illustrative and stylized: think cel-shading or flat shading with crisp outlines, minimal texture noise, and strong value separation.  This creates a unique visual identity suited to a browser game (lighter assets, shader simplicity) while emphasizing gameplay clarity.

## Transferable Mark of the Ninja Principles
Mark of the Ninja’s appeal lies in its **silhouette clarity and contrast**.  The ninja is rendered as a solid black figure in shadow, with only tattoos (red) or outline visible.  In light, he is fully colored to signal danger.  This “shape over detail” approach is key: small details exist only to accent the primary form, never to obscure it.  MoN also uses **controlled color and lighting**: the environment is mostly dark or monochrome, so any light or color (e.g. glowing eyes, illuminated switches) immediately draws attention and conveys game state.  Sound cues are visualized by simple ripples or icons, fitting the minimal aesthetic.  **Fog-of-war and shadow** techniques reveal or hide information (faded silhouettes in dark areas).  Although we won’t copy MoN’s setting, its principles – bold silhouettes, limited palette, and illustrative clarity – guide our style: every character and object should read instantly by shape and value alone.

## Translating 2D Stylization to 3D Football
Moving from MoN’s 2D side-scroller to a 3D football match, we must maintain the same principles in three dimensions.  A **stylized 3D rendering** (cel-shaded or simplified PBR) can mimic 2D clarity.  For example, use flat lighting layers (three-tone shading: shadow, midtone, highlight) instead of smooth gradients.  Outlines or edge highlights can preserve the graphic “drawn” look around characters and objects.  Camera perspective varies (wide, zoom), so we must ensure **silhouettes hold in 3D**: simple, exaggerated body shapes that read from any angle.  Depth cues and perspective distortion should not collapse form: long limbs or uniform pieces should not appear indistinct from behind.  Essentially, we apply the MoN idea of “silhouette readability” to 3D characters in motion.  Importantly, we **eschew photorealism**: textures will be flat or painterly, geometry low-to-medium poly, and detail only where it reinforces the design (e.g. a helmet crest, jersey number) without clutter.

## Player Silhouette & Shape Strategy
We will define **clear shape language** for player types (goalkeeper, defender, midfielder, forward, referee).  Following stylization best practices, shapes convey role: square/rectilinear volumes suggest stability and mass (e.g. defenders), rounder curves suggest agility or support (e.g. midfielders), and angular shapes hint at speed or aggression (e.g. strikers).  For example, a goalkeeper’s broad shoulders and crouch form a distinctive blocky silhouette, whereas forwards might have longer legs and lean posture.  All player models will share a consistent **stylized anatomy**: slightly caricatured limbs and torso proportions to emphasize key joints (knees, shoulders).  Silhouette tests at multiple distances (10m, 30m, 60m) in several poses (idle, running, kicking) will ensure each role reads as its class.  If a character “just looks like a humanoid with arms” in any pose, we adjust until their silhouette is unique.

## Player Individuality & Archetypes
Within each team, players must be distinguishable yet cohesive.  We will create **player archetypes**: a small set of body-types (e.g. tall slender, stocky, average), hair styles, and face/head shapes, each strongly readable by silhouette.  Variation can come from hairstyle (long vs short), accessories (headband, beard), and uniform stylings (sleeve colors, number patches) – but always in flat, graphic form.  Facial detail will be minimal: no fine features visible at gameplay distances.  Instead, helmets, hair, and crest shapes will carry identity.  Uniform designs will follow the color/value hierarchy (see below) so that one team’s players are immediately grouped by color and shape, while still showing variation.  Assign each individual a mix of attributes (body archetype + palette pattern) to create a library of plausible players.  Importantly, exaggerated proportions serve gameplay: for instance, defenders can be slightly bulkier (broad shapes) and strikers leaner (dynamic pose silhouettes), reinforcing roles through form.

## Geometry and Detail Level
Geometry will be **optimized for silhouettes**, not intricate mesh detail.  Character models use clean, bold surfaces: planes and cylinders rather than high-frequency sculpt.  Triangles will align with large muscle or cloth folds to preserve strong edge silhouettes.  We adopt a polygon budget that allows LOD downscaling for distant players (see LOD section).  Detail such as wrinkles, tiny logos, or complex patterns are avoided.  Instead, a simple color-block or emblem highlights and conveys information.  For example, a player’s crest might be represented by a solid shape or icon on chest rather than a textured logo.  This keeps the focus on silhouette and color contrast over texture noise.  The ball is similar: a clean, perhaps two-tone sphere with clear marking, plus a thick shadow and possibly a glow or motion trail when in flight, so it remains visible on grass.

## Kit and Team Readability
Team kits rely on **high-contrast color schemes** and patterns.  Each team’s primary color (and alternate, e.g. goalkeeper kit) is chosen for maximum difference.  We compress the palette to avoid noise: each jersey might use 2–3 values (body, trim, number) in one hue family or complement.  For example, Team A in blue/gold and Team B in red/white, with strong midtone separation.  We enforce *value compression* (stylization technique) so jerseys have blocky shadow/midtone/highlight rather than subtle shading.  Numbers and logos are bold silhouettes themselves (thick outlines on the design) so they remain legible at distance.  At a glance players are distinguished by color and shape (helmet vs no helmet, etc.), not fine detail.  This follows the “color script” approach: a palette guiding all design choices to avoid clashes.

## Color and Value System
The overall scene uses a **limited value range**.  The pitch might be a uniform mid-green (desaturated) to let players and ball pop.  Uniforms are generally more saturated than background, and ball is the brightest highlight (e.g. a white/yellow ball on green field).  We set strict rules: e.g. no two teams share a hue or brightness.  Key objects (goals, ball, referees) may have an accent color (e.g. neon) to stand out.  Under different lighting (day/night), these relationships hold: lighter values always draw the eye.  Dark areas (crowd, distant stands) are desaturated silhouettes themselves, with only pitch-line and player silhouettes visible, avoiding “visual noise” in backgrounds.  This value compression—using distinct steps for shadow, midtone, highlight—makes shapes readable by lighting alone.  We’ll also test in grayscale (low saturation) that teams and ball remain distinguishable (a standard stylized art check).

## Shading and Outline Techniques
We propose a **stylized-PBR or hybrid NPR pipeline** (see rendering stacks) rather than full photorealism.  Characters and the ball use flat to softly shaded surfaces; real-time lighting (from stadium lights) applies in broad strokes, not fine-grained physically-based specular.  Ideally, a cel-shader (with 2–3 color bands) is used for players, with possible outline rendering.  Outlines (thin black edges) are optional: if used, they must adapt to camera distance (thinner when close, thicker when far) to avoid “popping”.  For instance, use a screen-space post-process for consistent outline thickness.  NPR-style outlines significantly boost silhouette clarity, but they require performance budget.  If outline is too costly, the fallback is on extremely controlled shading: for example, a strong rim highlight on all players, and a gentle ambient occlusion (baked) that doesn’t reduce contrast.  In any case, the shading model is locked early to avoid mid-project shifts.  Enforce that even in partial light (stadium edges), a player’s form reads distinctly.

## Lighting and Shadow Strategy
Lighting is designed to **enhance silhouette and focus**.  We assume bright stadium lights (or daylight) so players cast clear shadows on the pitch.  To simplify, we can use stylized shadow maps: e.g. a single large light per side, with hard edges, so player shadows are crisp and consistent.  Controlled contrast means shadow on players may be a flat tone (dark region) without subtle gradients, emphasizing the shape.  For kickoffs or cutscenes, we can mimic MoN’s fog-of-war: edges of camera view or obscured zones get vignette/darkness so off-screen players aren’t distracting.  Ambient lighting is low-intensity to keep ball and players bright by comparison.  Ball shadow: a drop shadow or ground contact effect ensures it never “blends in” with pitch patterns.  If using bloom/glow, it is very subtle – perhaps a slight glow on bright uniforms or the ball under high light, never overwhelming.

## Ball Readability and VFX
The ball is critical: it must never be lost.  It should be the **brightest object** on screen except UI, with a distinct outline or glow.  A common trick is a thin outline or stroke (even white or black) around the ball sprite, so it separates from the green turf.  During high-speed play (shots, long passes), we can add a simple motion trail (a semi-transparent streak) or afterimage, akin to comics, to emphasize trajectory.  However, VFX must be restrained so as not to clutter (limited smoke or spark on contact only).  When ball is in the air, consider a subtle spotlight or brightened shading.  All these ensure the player’s eye locks onto the ball amidst 22 moving bodies.

## Pitch, Stadium, and Crowd Treatment
The **pitch** will be clean and uncluttered: a solid color or gentle gradient, with minimal pattern (maybe faint cross-hatching or simple lines).  Field markings (lines, center circle) should be thick and high-contrast (e.g. bright white or stadium glow) so boundaries are always clear.  Grass texture, if any, is very low-frequency; busy grass patterns can hide the ball and feet.  Surrounding **stadium** is mostly silhouette: distant crowd as darkened shapes, perhaps with area-specific team-color accents (like blocks of color for fans, but blurred).  Avoid rendering individual spectators.  Static props (scoreboards, corner flags) follow the stylized palette (e.g. flat red flags with black outlines).  In close-up modes, we can add some 3D detail to benches/goals but keep them uniformly colored.  This keeps performance manageable and ensures focus remains on players/ball.

## Animation Readability Principles
Player animations must **clearly telegraph actions**.  Stylization allows slight exaggeration: e.g. a longer follow-through on kicks, a higher jump when heading.  Key movements (sprinting, turning, tackling) should use strong, easily read poses.  For example, lean the torso more in a sprint, or raise arms in a Tackle animation.  The principle is akin to “pose readability” in character animation: every major frame should silhouette the action (anticipation pose, key pose, follow-through).  We should avoid very subtle animations that are invisible in play.  Likely we’ll use more frames for ball kicks and headers (so the ball does not “jump” each frame), but can simplify idle and jogging cycles.  Slow motions (braking, dribbling) get clarity via maybe a slow-motion smear or emphasis on stance change.  All transitions (e.g. turning) should be crisp; blended animations that muddy posture should be minimized.  Essentially, the **animation style complements art style**: it’s cartoonish, not generic mo-cap.

## Gameplay-Camera Constraints
Since camera may zoom or switch (broadcast angles, replays, close-ups), character design must hold at multiple scales.  We assume a default camera similar to TV soccer (slightly elevated behind field line), where players at half-field may be ~20 meters from camera.  We will simulate this: test models at typical gameplay distances to ensure silhouettes are still identifiable.  Farther zooms (wide-view) need simpler LOD (see below).  Near-camera views (goal replays) allow more detail (face marks, jersey texture) but keep them minimal.  If possible, constrain camera to not go so high that players become tiny dots; add minimal HUD cues (arrows or name-tags) when far.  In short, design with the camera in mind: players should not rely on makeup or small emblems to be readable.

## LOD and Asset-Pipeline Recommendations
A multi-LOD strategy is essential for performance.  **LOD0** (close) has full character mesh and cel-shader.  **LOD1** (mid-distance) simplifies geometry (e.g. fewer limbs/rings) and textures (2-color jersey only).  **LOD2** (far) might replace player with a simple colored capsule or flat billboard silhouette (just a circle/head and block body).  Automated LOD generation tools should be used.  The pipeline: model base mesh → high-detail sculpt (for outline bake if needed) → retopo for real-time LODs → UV/textures with compressed (8-bit normal, single albedo) → engine asset.  All character assets share a common rig and skeleton to reuse animations.  Uniforms and skins are textured variations of a template with different masks.  Critical visuals (silhouette, color-blocks) are reviewed at each step.  Textures are small (e.g. 512×512) to fit browser memory.  Shaders are kept simple (unlit or single-blend lighting) to run fast on typical devices.  Baking pre-lighting or ambient occlusion into textures (rather than real-time) can help fidelity without cost.

## Procedural Player Variation
To maximize variety with minimal manual art, we use procedural/parametric variation.  For example, define a set of head meshes (several hairdos, noses), body meshes (short/tall, muscled/slim), and color templates.  Then generate players by random combinations, respecting team colors and role archetype.  Texture atlases pack all team colors; shaders swap color by index.  Optionally, integrate a simple face texture generator (or use few cartoon face variants).  Uniform numbers can be procedurally placed from a digit font.  A ruleset ensures coherence (e.g. star players bigger size, certain hairstyles etc.).  This pipeline supports *configurable properties*: designers can tweak team palettes, outline thickness, animation exaggeration, etc., via scripts or in-engine parameters, keeping production manageable.

## Visual Evaluation Criteria
For ongoing validation, we propose both **objective** and **perceptual** metrics.  Objective tests include the silhouette recognition tests from stylized art practice: render characters (and ball) in monochrome at various scales/angles and check that team and role remain distinguishable.  We can script silhouette image comparisons (pixel overlap, shape matching) as automated gates.  Color contrast metrics (like ΔE difference) ensure team colors vs field always exceed a threshold.  Another metric is *visual clutter score*: e.g. counting texture “noise” or crowd detail in screen.  Perceptually, we might use timed pop-out tasks: can players or the ball be quickly identified in sample frames?  These can be done as design reviews or simple user tests.  Ultimately, any design must pass the stylized art bible rules: clear silhouettes, compressed value range, and no style drift.  (Connected sources from stylized-art literature provide guidance but specific implementation is project-driven.)

## Visual Experiments & References
We will create a **reference scene catalog** to explore style decisions.  For instance:
- A fully dark (night) stadium scene vs day match to compare silhouette and color usage.
- Close-up of two players colliding or competing, to test overlapping silhouettes.
- Zoomed-out full-field view to ensure 22 players and ball are readable.
- Experiment with outline on/off, or variable outline thickness with camera distance.
- Colorblind-mode preview (game engine filter) to check team differentiation.
- Lighting variations (e.g. strong side light vs top-down) to assess shadow clarity.

These experiments, documented with images and notes, become a mini-style guide.  We also collect real-time references (other stylized games: *Overwatch* for color use, *Valorant* for outlines, *Fortnite* for LOD, etc.) and case studies (e.g. Genshin’s shader breakdown).  Results from each experiment feed back into the art spec (e.g. “outline must be enabled” or “jersey contrast too low”).

## DECIDE NOW / DEFER
Certain design choices should be decided early: e.g. **Rendering stack** (stylized PBR vs full NPR vs hybrid) locks tools and skills. Based on constraints, a stylized-PBR pipeline with simple outlines seems safest (lower tech risk than full custom NPR). Team color themes should also be settled upfront to guide art. Deferred items might include exact polygon budgets and postprocess VFX (tune after prototyping). Whether to include high-detail faces can wait until performance testing. Ultimately: *Decide now* – use a limited color palette, stylized lighting, high-contrast silhouettes. *Defer* – e.g. the precise shader models or crowd polycount until prototype reveals performance.

## Originality Considerations
To ensure an original identity (not just “ninja soccer”), we avoid copying any existing game’s look. While inspired by MoN’s silhouette strategy, we will *not* mimic its subject matter or environment.  Our soccer art should not feel like a simple port of an existing sports title’s style.  For example, rather than generic anime players, we might incorporate subtle novel cues (unique uniform emblem style, quirky character silhouettes, or a distinctive artistic motif in the stadium).  All character designs are original, and any illustrative elements (logos, icons) are custom-made.  We will document the style guide to prevent drift into realism or pop-art clichés.  This includes trademarking any unique visual twist (e.g. a bold outline style or exaggerated body feature) so the game’s look is recognizable on its own merits.

## Open Questions
- **Shading model**: Exactly which shaders do we implement? (Cel shade bands vs minimal PBR) – requires prototype test.
- **Outline on/off trade-offs**: If outlines cost too much, can the style survive without them?
- **Camera angle presets**: Will we allow dynamic camera (zoom/rotate) or fixed broadcast angles? How does that affect LOD needs?
- **Crowd representation**: Could a low-poly crowd silhouette (like stripes) add atmosphere without noise?
- **Color palette**: Which team color pairs are both attractive and highly legible?
- **Performance on target browsers**: Need to verify the chosen shader complexity and polycount run smoothly on expected hardware.

Each of these should be investigated in tandem with engineering tests, but the art approach outlined above provides the framework for those decisions.
